Career Pathfinder

An AI app that turns "what do I want to do?" into a researched, multi-year career map with the decision points you will face and a timed plan for each skill you will need.

2. Motivation

Who has the problem. First, college students choosing a first career; second, early-career workers changing careers. Both make a decision with ten-year consequences using fragmented information.

  • The stakes are high and persistent. 52% of recent four-year graduates are underemployed a year after graduating (in a job that doesn't need their degree), and 73% of those are still underemployed ten years later [1].
  • Students change course often. About one in three bachelor's students changes major within three years [2]. Americans born 1957–64 held 12.7 jobs between ages 18 and 56 [3], so career changes are the norm.
  • Help is scarce. The median US career center has 1,381 students per professional staff member [4], and only 30% of graduates rated the career services office helpful or very helpful [5].
  • The alternative is expensive. Private career coaches charge roughly $75–$225 per hour [6].

How people cope today. They piece the answer together themselves: occupation profiles (O*NET, BLS), salary sites (Glassdoor, Indeed), LinkedIn profiles of people a few steps ahead, and increasingly ChatGPT. Each answers one slice of the question. None shows where a first job leads, where the forks are, what each fork pays, and what to learn when. Building that picture for three candidate careers takes hours (our estimate: 3–6 hours of reading), and most people never get to the "what should I do this quarter" level.

Why care. A bad first step compounds: underemployment is sticky [1], and switching later costs years of earnings. A tool that makes the long view cheap and fast helps students before they commit. It also lets career centers spend scarce counsellor time on judgment, not on research.

What Career Pathfinder adds. Being honest: a skilled user could get most of this content from ChatGPT in an hour of careful prompting. What is new is the packaging into a guided, repeatable five-step workflow that takes about five minutes:

  1. A visual, branching career map per job, with years on the x-axis, pay and requirements at every role, explicit decision points, and a slider that compares careers at any year.
  2. Grounded numbers. Titles and pay come from live searches of BLS, Indeed, LinkedIn, Glassdoor and others. Every citation links to a real search result, and the model can only cite sources by number.
  3. A timing-aware plan, enforced in code: skills are scheduled shortly before the role that needs them, not "learn everything now". It also includes moves the person can make inside their current job.
  4. Personalized from a resume (contact details stripped locally first), with skill levels pre-filled. Live job openings for any role on the path.
  5. Guardrails a chatbot doesn't have: a career safety filter, a hard search budget, read-only tools, and output repair. See section 6.

How they are organized

This is a pipeline with a central harness. Agents never call each other or the web directly. The harness (FastAPI server) runs every agent, executes its searches in parallel within the budget, validates its JSON output, and hands the result to the next agent:

The Career Agent's search findings are handed to the Coach Agent (dashed blue arrow), so prerequisites are grounded in the same evidence as the map. A separate safety classifier and the O*NET title mapper are small helper calls, not agents.

Model choice

All agents use gpt-5.6-luna at low reasoning effort. I first built the Career and Coach Agents on gpt-5.6-terra, the larger model. A blind comparison (section 5) showed terra writes somewhat better maps, but it costs 10× more per token and was 2.5× slower (25s vs 10s per map). For a student tool where a run must take minutes and cost cents, luna plus code-level quality checks was the better trade. Low effort cut output tokens by a third compared with luna's default effort (1,638 vs 2,429 per map, averaged over 3 jobs) and saved about 5 seconds per map.

One real run, step by step

Persona: a customer-service team lead in Atlanta considering a move (the fictional resume in samples/). All timings were measured in the app on this run.

1
Starting point (Resume Agent, 2.2 s). The resume is read on the computer; name, email, phone, address and links are removed before the AI sees it. Title, years, degree and 12 skills are extracted and pre-fill the form.
2
Positions (Position Agent, 3 s for questions, 17 s for matches). The agent asks three questions; several answers can be picked. It then ranks 10 jobs. The person selects Customer Success, Customer Experience and Customer Service Manager. Each selection quietly starts its career research in the background.
3
Career paths (Career Agent; 3 maps ready 0.4 s after the click because they were built while the person was reading; 10–13 s for the first map otherwise). The chosen map drew on 16 sources from 2 searches. The slider shows where the person could be at year 8 in each career. They choose Customer Success, pick the management track at both decision points, and add a note: "SaaS company in Atlanta, ideally Mailchimp or Salesloft".
4
Skills (Coach Agent, 12.5 s). 21 requirements, each tagged with the year and role that first needs it. 19 of the 21 levels were pre-filled from the resume (e.g. "From your profile: Zendesk and Salesforce Service Cloud"). Graded skills show the target; yes/no items (degrees, required experience) are simply "I have this / Not yet".
5
Your plan (Coach Agent, 15.9 s; openings 11.4 s). The plan covers trajectory and pay ($65–95k at year 0 to $170–280k as Director), moves inside the current job, the next 90 days, and phases that target the named employers. A live search found 7 Customer Success Manager postings in Atlanta (Finastra, Intuit, Samsara…).

What I did to keep cost (and time) down

  • Smaller model, low effort: luna instead of terra (10× cheaper per token); low reasoning effort (about half the output tokens).
  • Search only where it matters: job matching uses the free bundled O*NET database instead of searches; the skills step reuses the Career Agent's research; 2 searches per career, each capped at one tool call (max_tool_calls).
  • Cache and de-duplicate: fixed queries per job, so re-mapping the same job costs $0 (measured 0.2 s). A search already running is awaited, never repeated.
  • Short, structured outputs: strict JSON schemas, length rules (e.g. "description ≤ 15 words"), and the plan written as two parallel halves.
  • Hard caps per run: 15 searches, 80 model calls and 600k tokens (section 6).

Did cost cutting hurt quality? Yes, somewhat. Evidence:

Same 3 jobs, identical search results, three model settings. A blind judge (terra at default effort; maps shown in random order, with the research as ground truth) compared the original setup with the current one:

So the cheaper setup was 10× cheaper and 2.5× faster, but scored about one point lower. Both failure types were specific and fixable, so I fixed them in the harness rather than paying for the bigger model:

  • A code-level quality gate: if a role repeats a title already on its route, the Career Agent gets one targeted correction round. In a re-test of 4 maps it fired once and fixed it ("Manager of Customer Success"), with 0 repeats left.
  • Prompt rules: no pivots into a different licensed profession without naming the new licence.

The judge sample is small (3 jobs), so these numbers show the direction, not a precise measurement. A hybrid (terra only for the map) would cost about $0.18 per run and is a next step (section 8).

Illustrative scale. A university with 10,000 students where each runs it 3 times a year would spend about $3,600 a year in API costs. If 10% of them (1,000 students) used it in place of the 3–6 hours of manual research estimated above, that is roughly 3,000–6,000 hours of student time saved. Counsellors would also start conversations from a concrete plan instead of a blank page.

How I would measure real impact (pilot): 15–20 students at a career center. Track time to a first written plan, confidence before/after (5-point scale), and the counsellor's rating of plan quality, compared with a group that uses existing resources.

8. Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • No real-user validation. All quality evidence comes from my runs and a model judge, not from students or counsellors.
  • Salary precision. Pay comes from web search and model estimates. Official BLS wage data isn't bundled because BLS blocks automated downloads; a script to add it is included.
  • Map quality with the cheaper model. It was about one point lower than terra in a small blind test; the repeated-title gate fixes one failure type, but not every one.
  • Job openings depend on the search index. Many LinkedIn/Indeed postings aren't indexed, so results lean on Glassdoor and ZipRecruiter, plus direct LinkedIn/Indeed search links.
  • US-centric and English-only. O*NET, BLS and the salary sites are US sources. Scanned (image-only) resumes can't be read.
  • No accounts. Progress lives in the browser or a downloaded file, and the search budget resets per run.
